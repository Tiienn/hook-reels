# Hook patterns

Names to use when labelling a hook. Each has the mechanism in one line and a way to adapt it.
The "seen in" examples come from reels this skill was tested on; the mechanisms are the
creators' explanations and common marketing guidance, not measured facts.

## Visual hooks (work muted)

| Pattern | Mechanism | Adapt it |
|---|---|---|
| **Anticipation** | Show something about to happen; the viewer waits for the outcome. Seen in: car driving off with the fuel hose attached, a pole falling toward a car, an axe coming down on a rock. | Open on the moment before the result in your niche: the cake about to be cut, the before-photo sliding away. |
| **Unfinished countdown / open loop** | Start a process with an obvious ending, then never show it; the message plays while the viewer waits. Seen in: a lit fuse that is pocketed while the seller talks about hats. | Start a timer, a pour, a reveal; deliver your point in the middle; resolve at the very end or not at all. |
| **Hypnotic motion** | Smooth, rhythmic movement holds the eye. Seen in: a metal tool spinning upright on a table. | Loopable motion relevant to you: kneading, sanding, a machine cycle, a satisfying wipe. |
| **Unexpected visual** | Something that does not belong in the scene breaks the scroll pattern. | Put the odd object or odd action in frame one, explain later. |

## Spoken / text hooks

## Adapting a pattern to a niche

1. Keep the mechanism, swap the subject. The fuse becomes whatever "obviously about to finish" looks like in the user's world.
2. Make the first frame carry it without sound: reels autoplay muted, so pair every spoken hook with on-screen text of the same line.
3. Put the payoff where the pattern needs it. Anticipation pays off fast (2-4 s). Open loops pay off at the end.
4. Write the hook line under about 10 words. Offer two or three variants per pattern.
5. Do not promise what the video does not deliver; a hook that misleads costs watch time later.
